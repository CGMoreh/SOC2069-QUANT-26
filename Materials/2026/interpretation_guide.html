Interpretation Guide: Saying What Your Results Mean

For each kind of output you will meet in this module, this guide gives:

  • what to look at in the JASP output,
  • a model write-up you can adapt for your own report, and
  • common phrasings to avoid, with better alternatives.

All the examples use the course datasets, so you can reproduce every number yourself.

ImportantThree rules for every write-up
  1. Say what was measured: name the survey, year, question and scale, not just the variable name.
  2. Give direction and size: “higher” is not enough. Say how much higher, in units a reader understands.
  3. Describe associations, not causes: regression on survey data shows how variables go together. Use “is associated with”, “is higher among”, “predicts”, not “causes”, “increases”, “leads to” or “the effect of”.

1. Describing one variable

Example: social trust (ppltrst, 0-10) in the UK sample of the European Social Survey, Round 11 (gb-ess11-trust.sav).

Look at: valid N, mean, median, mode, standard deviation, and the distribution plot.

In the UK sample of the European Social Survey (2023-24, n = 1,680), respondents rated how far most people can be trusted at 5.4 on average on a scale from 0 (“you can’t be too careful”) to 10 (“most people can be trusted”) (SD = 2.3). The median was 6 and the most common answer 7, so more respondents leaned towards trust than away from it, with a smaller tail of very low scores pulling the mean slightly below the median.

✗ Avoid ✓ Better
“The mean of ppltrst is 5.4.” Name the concept and the scale: “average trust was 5.4 on a 0-10 scale”.
“Most people trust others.” Say what the numbers show: “the most common answer was 7 out of 10”.
Reporting the mean of a nominal variable Use percentages and the mode for categorical variables.

2. Two categorical variables: contingency tables

Example: SocTrust by sex, British Social Attitudes 2010 (column percentages).

Men Women
Most people can be trusted 51.4% 40.7%
Can’t be too careful 48.6% 59.3%
n 461 599

Look at: percentages within each category of the predictor (column percentages if the predictor is in the columns), and the size of the difference.

In the 2010 British Social Attitudes survey, men were more likely than women to say that most people can be trusted (51% vs 41%), a difference of about 11 percentage points.

✗ Avoid ✓ Better
“237 men and 244 women trust people.” Compare percentages, not counts: the groups are different sizes.
“Women are 11% less trusting.” “11 percentage points lower” (40.7% is about 21% lower than 51.4%: not the same thing).
Using row percentages to compare men and women Percentages must add to 100 within each group you compare.

3. A scale variable across groups

Example: ppltrst (0-10) by gender, ESS Round 11, UK.

Look at: the mean or median in each group, the spread, and the box plot.

Men in the UK ESS sample reported slightly higher trust than women (mean 5.5 vs 5.3 on the 0-10 scale; medians 6 and 5). The distributions overlap heavily: the gap between the group means is small compared with the spread within each group (SD of about 2.3 points).

4. Two scale variables: correlation

Example: income inequality and social trust across 23 rich countries (pickett2009).

Look at: the scatter plot first, then Pearson’s r (direction and strength).

Across 23 economically developed countries, income inequality and social trust were strongly negatively correlated (r = −0.66): in more unequal countries, a smaller share of people said that most people can be trusted.

✗ Avoid ✓ Better
“Inequality reduces trust.” “Inequality is negatively associated with trust.”
“Unequal countries have untrusting citizens.” The data are country-level: they describe countries, not individuals (the ecological fallacy).
“r = −0.66, so 66% of trust is explained.” The share of variance explained is r² ≈ 0.44.

5. Simple linear regression

Example: Trust (% trusting) regressed on Income inequality (income quintile share ratio), pickett2009.

(n = 23) Unstandardized R²
(Intercept) 77.06 0.441
Income inequality −6.54

Look at: the Unstandardized coefficient of the predictor, in the units of both variables; R².

A simple linear regression shows that countries with higher income inequality tend to have lower social trust: each one-unit higher income quintile share ratio (for example, the richest fifth earning 8 rather than 7 times as much as the poorest fifth) is associated with a share of trusting people that is about 6.5 percentage points lower. Income inequality alone accounts for 44% of the variation in trust across these countries (R² = 0.44).

The intercept (77.06) is the predicted trust when inequality is 0, which is impossible (the ratio cannot be below 1), so it does not need interpreting.

6. Multiple linear regression

Example: the same model, adding Homicides (per 100,000 population).

(n = 23) Unstandardized 95% CI p
Income inequality −7.15 −10.98 to −3.32 < .001
Homicides 0.17 −0.33 to 0.68 .483
R² 0.455

Look at: how the main coefficient changes once controls are added; each coefficient’s CI and p-value; R².

Adjusting for homicide rates, income inequality remained negatively associated with trust: among countries with similar homicide rates, a one-unit higher quintile share ratio was associated with about 7 percentage points lower trust (95% CI −11.0 to −3.3; p < .001). Homicide rates were not reliably associated with trust once inequality was taken into account (b = 0.17, 95% CI −0.33 to 0.68; p = .48), and adding them improved the model’s explanatory power only marginally (R² from 0.44 to 0.46).

✗ Avoid ✓ Better
“Controlling for homicides, inequality’s effect is −7.15.” “Adjusting for homicide rates, inequality is associated with …” (no “effect”)
“Homicides increase trust (b = 0.17).” The CI includes 0 and p = .48: the data cannot tell the direction of this association.
“Inequality matters more because −7.15 > 0.17.” The variables have different units: compare standardized coefficients for relative strength.

7. A categorical predictor

Example: trust (0-10) regressed on age group (16-24 vs 25+, reference = 25+) and sex, Community Life Survey 2023-24 (England).

(n = 50,706) Unstandardized 95% CI p
Age group (16-24) −0.59 −0.65 to −0.53 < .001
Sex (Male) −0.01 −0.05 to 0.02 .460

Look at: which category is the reference; each coefficient is a difference from it.

Adults aged 16-24 rated their trust in most people 0.6 points lower on the 0-10 scale than those aged 25 and over, holding sex constant (95% CI −0.65 to −0.53; p < .001). There was no meaningful difference between men and women.

8. Logistic regression

Example: SocTrust (1 = “Most people can be trusted”) regressed on age and sex, British Social Attitudes 2010.

(n = 1,060) Estimate Odds ratio 95% CI (OR) p
Age (years) 0.006 1.006 0.9995 to 1.013 .069
Sex (Female) −0.437 0.646 0.505 to 0.825 < .001
Nagelkerke R² 0.019

Look at: which outcome category is coded 1; the Odds Ratio column; then translate into predicted probabilities (conditional estimates plot).

A logistic regression shows that, among respondents of the same age, women had about 35% lower odds than men of saying that most people can be trusted (OR = 0.65, 95% CI 0.51 to 0.83; p < .001). In probability terms, the model predicts that 50% of 40-year-old men but 39% of 40-year-old women say most people can be trusted, a gap of about 11 percentage points that is similar at every age. Older respondents were slightly more trusting (about 0.6% higher odds per year of age, or 6% per decade), but this association was not statistically significant (95% CI 0.9995 to 1.013, which JASP rounds to 1.000; p = .069). Age and sex together explain very little of the variation in trust (Nagelkerke R² = 0.02).

✗ Avoid ✓ Better
“Women are 35% less likely to trust.” “Women’s odds of trusting are 35% lower”, and give the probability gap (11 points).
“The odds ratio is 0.646, so women’s probability is 0.646.” Odds ratios compare odds, not probabilities.
Interpreting the log-odds Estimate directly as a size Use the sign of the Estimate for direction; use the odds ratio or probabilities for size.
Not saying which category is coded 1 “The outcome is coded 1 for ‘most people can be trusted’.”

9. Uncertainty: confidence intervals and p-values

Look at: the 95% CI (the range of population values compatible with the data) and the p-value (how surprising the estimate would be if the true association were zero).

The odds of trusting were 35% lower for women (OR = 0.65, 95% CI 0.51 to 0.83; p < .001). Because the interval lies entirely below 1, the data are not compatible with the absence of a sex difference in the population.

A non-significant result is not evidence of no difference. In the British Social Attitudes 2024, 16-24-year-olds scored 0.16 points lower than older adults on the 0-10 trust scale (95% CI −0.71 to 0.40; p = .58), based on only 78 young respondents.

The BSA 2024 shows a small difference in the same direction that is not statistically significant. Its confidence interval is wide and includes both no difference and the 0.6-point gap found in the much larger Community Life Survey, so the BSA 2024 cannot settle whether young people’s trust is lower.

✗ Avoid ✓ Better
“p = .58, so there is no difference.” “The difference is not statistically significant; the interval is compatible with no difference and with a sizeable one.”
“p < .001, so the effect is large.” Significance is about precision, not size: report the coefficient and its interval.
“There is a 95% probability that the true value is in the CI.” “Intervals built this way capture the true value in 95% of repeated samples.”
“p = 0.000” Report “p < .001”.
