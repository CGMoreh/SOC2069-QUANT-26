Week 1 Self-check Quiz: Measurement and description

Ten short questions to check your understanding after the Week 1 lecture. It takes about 10 minutes. Try each question before opening the answer. The examples use the same data as the lecture and Worksheet 1 (mainly the UK sample of the European Social Survey 2023-24, gb-ess11-trust.sav).

1. Level of measurement

Which level of measurement is age in years (agea)?

  1. Nominal
  2. Ordinal
  3. Interval
  4. Ratio
TipAnswer

d. Age has equal steps between values and a true zero, so ratios make sense (a 60-year-old is twice as old as a 30-year-old). In JASP, interval and ratio variables are both treated as Scale.

2. The right statistic

Which summary makes sense for a nominal variable such as sex (gndr)?

  1. The mean
  2. The median
  3. The mode and the percentage in each category
  4. The standard deviation
TipAnswer

c. Nominal categories have no order, so only counts, percentages and the mode are meaningful. JASP will happily calculate a “mean” of the codes 1 and 2, but it means nothing.

3. Mean, median, mode

For trust in the ESS (0-10), the mean is 5.40, the median 6 and the mode 7. What does this pattern suggest?

  1. The distribution is perfectly symmetric
  2. A tail of low scores pulls the mean below the median (mild left skew)
  3. A few very high scores pull the mean up (right skew)
  4. The data contain errors
TipAnswer

b. More people answer above the middle of the scale than below, but a tail of very low answers (0 to 2, chosen by 13% of respondents) drags the mean down. In a right-skewed distribution such as income, it’s the other way round: the mean is above the median.

4. Mean or median?

Nine households have incomes (in £000s) of 18, 22, 25, 28, 31, 34, 38, 45 and 400. Which figure best describes a “typical” household?

  1. The mean, 71
  2. The median, 31
  3. The range, 382
  4. The maximum, 400
TipAnswer

b. The single very high income pulls the mean (71) above eight of the nine households. The median (31) is barely affected, which is why incomes and wealth are usually reported as medians.

5. Percent of what?

In the BSA 2010, 484 respondents said “most people can be trusted”, out of 3,297 people in the file. Only 1,081 were asked the question, and 17 of them answered “Don’t know” or refused. Which percentage best describes the share of trusting answers?

  1. 484 / 3,297 = 14.7%
  2. 484 / 1,081 = 44.8%
  3. 484 / 1,064 = 45.5%
  4. All three are equally good
TipAnswer

c. The question was only asked in one version of the questionnaire, so the 2,216 people never asked must be left out of the denominator. “Don’t know” and refusals are not substantive answers either, so the clearest figure uses the 1,064 substantive answers. Always check what the denominator of a percentage is.

6. Ratios

In the BSA 2010, 484 respondents said “most people can be trusted” and 580 said “you can’t be too careful”. What is the ratio of trusting to careful answers?

  1. 0.45
  2. 0.83
  3. 1.20
  4. 45.5%
TipAnswer

b. 484 ÷ 580 = 0.83: about 8 trusting answers for every 10 careful ones. (0.45 is the proportion trusting, and 1.20 is the ratio the other way round.) In Week 4 this ratio reappears as the odds of trusting.

7. Standard deviation

The standard deviation of trust in the ESS is 2.30. What does it describe?

  1. The typical respondent’s trust score
  2. The typical distance of an answer from the mean
  3. The difference between the highest and lowest answers
  4. The share of respondents within one point of the mean
TipAnswer

b. A typical answer lies about 2.3 points above or below the mean of 5.4. The difference between the highest and lowest answers is the range (here 10).

8. The box plot

In a box plot of ESS trust, the box runs from 4 to 7 and the line inside it is at 6. What do these numbers show?

  1. The minimum, maximum and mean
  2. The 25th percentile, the 75th percentile and the median
  3. The mean ± one standard deviation
  4. The two most common answers and the average
TipAnswer

b. The box covers the middle 50% of respondents (the interquartile range, 7 − 4 = 3), and the line marks the median.

9. Measuring the same concept

Six British surveys asked about trust in “most people”. Depending on the survey, the share giving a trusting answer ranged from about 37% to 48% (11 percentage points apart), and two 0-10 scales had means of 4.3 and 6.1. What is the main lesson?

  1. Trust changed dramatically between surveys
  2. One of the surveys must have made an error
  3. Question wording, answer options and survey design shape the measured level of trust
  4. Social trust cannot be measured
TipAnswer

c. The surveys word the question differently, offer different answer options (two options, a middle category, an unoffered “depends”, 0-10 scales with different labels), use different modes and cover different populations. Levels of trust should only be compared across surveys with great care; comparisons within one survey are much safer.

10. Hidden missing values

In the BSA 2024 file, the 0-10 trust question has the extra values 12 (“Can’t choose”) and 99 (“Prefer not to answer”), which are not defined as missing. What happens if you calculate the mean straight away?

  1. Nothing: JASP ignores non-answers automatically
  2. The mean is too high, because 12 and 99 are averaged in as if they were scores
  3. The mean is too low
  4. JASP refuses to calculate a mean
TipAnswer

b. Unless they are set as missing, JASP treats 12 and 99 as valid values and includes them in the mean. Always look at a frequency table, compare values with labels, and set non-answers as missing first (Workshop 4 shows how).
