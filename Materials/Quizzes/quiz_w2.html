Week 2 Self-check Quiz: Comparisons and associations

Ten short questions to check your understanding after the Week 2 lecture. It takes about 10 minutes. Try each question before opening the answer. The examples use the same data as the lecture and Worksheet 2.

1. Choosing the tool

You want to describe how trust on the ESS 0-10 scale differs between men and women. Which tool fits?

  1. A contingency table with row percentages
  2. Group means and medians, shown in box plots
  3. A scatter plot with a correlation
  4. A frequency table of trust
TipAnswer

b. One variable is categorical (sex) and one is treated as scale (trust), so we compare the distribution of trust across the groups. Contingency tables are for two categorical variables; scatter plots for two scale variables.

2. Outcome and explanatory variables

In a study of age and social trust, which variable is the outcome?

  1. Age
  2. Social trust
  3. Either, it makes no difference
  4. Neither: both are explanatory
TipAnswer

b. Age can plausibly shape trust, but trust cannot change someone’s age. For many pairs (e.g. volunteering and trust) the direction is less obvious, and theory has to decide.

3. Column percentages

In the BSA 2010, 51.7% of men and 40.7% of women say most people can be trusted. These are column percentages with sex in the columns. What question do they answer?

  1. What share of trusting respondents are men?
  2. Are men or women more likely to say most people can be trusted?
  3. How many respondents trust most people?
  4. Is the difference statistically significant?
TipAnswer

b. Column percentages compare the groups in the columns: men are about 11 percentage points more likely to trust. Row percentages would answer question a (and only describe who the trusting are, which depends on how many men and women were sampled).

4. Percentage points

Trust is 51.7% among men and 40.7% among women. Which description is correct?

  1. Women are 11% less trusting
  2. Women are 11 percentage points less likely to trust
  3. Women are 21% as trusting as men
  4. The gap is 0.11%
TipAnswer

b. The difference between two percentages is measured in percentage points. In relative terms, 40.7% is about 21% lower than 51.7%, which is a different statement: always say which one you mean.

5. Reading a box plot

In the ESS, the box plots of trust for men and women both run from 4 to 7, with medians of 6 (men) and 5 (women). What can you conclude?

  1. Men and women have identical trust
  2. Men trust slightly more on average, but the two distributions overlap almost completely
  3. All men trust more than all women
  4. Women’s trust varies more because their median is lower
TipAnswer

b. The middle 50% of both groups covers the same range; only the medians (and the means, 5.54 vs 5.27) differ slightly. A gap between groups should always be judged against the spread within them.

6. Correlation

The correlation between income inequality and trust across 23 rich countries is r = −0.66. What does it mean?

  1. Inequality causes 66% of the variation in trust
  2. More unequal countries tend to have lower trust, and the association is fairly strong
  3. Every unequal country has low trust
  4. 66% of countries are unequal
TipAnswer

b. The sign gives the direction (negative: higher inequality, lower trust) and the size gives the strength. There are clear exceptions (e.g. the USA and New Zealand trust more than their inequality would suggest), and a correlation says nothing about cause.

7. Correlation and curves

A scatter plot shows a clear U-shaped relationship, yet Pearson’s r is almost exactly 0. Why?

  1. There must be an error in the calculation
  2. r only measures straight-line (linear) association
  3. r is always close to zero for curves with many points
  4. The two variables are unrelated
TipAnswer

b. Pearson’s r captures linear association only. That’s why you should always look at the scatter plot before relying on a correlation.

8. One influential case

In the 2024 Spirit Level data (43 countries), the correlation between the income quintile ratio and trust is −0.15, but −0.58 without China. What does this show?

  1. China’s data must be wrong
  2. One unusual case can change a correlation a lot
  3. The 2024 data contradict the 2009 data
  4. The quintile ratio is not a measure of inequality
TipAnswer

b. China combines by far the highest measured inequality with high measured trust, which flattens the line. Look for unusual cases in the scatter plot and report how much they matter.

9. Levels of analysis

Unequal countries tend to have lower trust. Can we conclude that poorer individuals trust less?

  1. Yes, that’s what the correlation means
  2. No: country-level data describe countries, not individuals
  3. Yes, if the correlation is strong enough
  4. No, because the correlation is negative
TipAnswer

b. Drawing conclusions about individuals from group-level data is the ecological fallacy. To study individuals we need individual-level survey data.

10. Explaining an association

Which of these explanations for the inequality-trust correlation can the correlation itself rule out?

  1. Inequality erodes trust
  2. Trust reduces inequality (trusting societies support redistribution)
  3. Something else, such as history or institutions, drives both
  4. None of them
TipAnswer

d. A correlation is compatible with all three stories. To weigh them we need theory, other variables (Week 3: multiple regression lets us adjust for some third factors) and ideally data over time.
