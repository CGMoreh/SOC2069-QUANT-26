Week 4 Self-check Quiz: Modelling dichotomous outcomes

Ten short questions to check your understanding after the Week 4 lecture. It takes about 10 minutes. Try each question before opening the answer. The examples use the same data as the lecture and Worksheet 4 (British Social Attitudes 2010: SocTrust modelled on age and sex).

1. Which outcome?

Which of these outcome variables calls for logistic rather than linear regression?

  1. Trust in most people, measured on a 0-10 scale (ESS ppltrst)
  2. The percentage of a country’s population who say most people can be trusted
  3. Whether the respondent voted in the last general election (yes/no)
  4. The number of close friends a respondent has
TipAnswer

c. It has only two possible values, so we model the probability of “yes”. The 0-10 scale (a) and the country percentage (b) can be treated as numeric scales; a count (d) is numeric too.

2. From probability to odds

30% of a sample say most people can be trusted. What are the odds of trusting?

  1. 0.30
  2. 0.43
  3. 2.33
  4. 0.70
TipAnswer

b. Odds = p ÷ (1 − p) = 0.3 ÷ 0.7 ≈ 0.43, or 3 trusting for every 7 who are not. (2.33 is the odds of not trusting.)

3. A coefficient of zero

A logistic regression Estimate is 0. What does that mean?

  1. The predicted probability is 0
  2. The odds ratio is 1: the predictor makes no difference to the odds
  3. The predictor perfectly predicts the outcome
  4. Nobody in the sample is in the category coded 1
TipAnswer

b. Estimates are on the log-odds scale, and e⁰ = 1. An odds ratio of 1 means the odds are the same whatever the value of the predictor.

4. Reading an estimate

JASP reports an Estimate of 0.693 for a predictor Sex (Female). What is the odds ratio, and what does it mean?

  1. 0.693: women’s odds are about 31% lower than men’s
  2. 2.0: women’s odds are twice men’s
  3. 1.693: women are 69% more likely than men to be in the outcome category
  4. You cannot tell without the p-value
TipAnswer

b. The odds ratio is e0.693 ≈ 2.0, so women’s odds are about twice men’s. Option c confuses odds with probability (and uses the wrong conversion). JASP shows the Odds Ratio column by default, so you rarely need to do this by hand.

5. Odds ratios in words

In the BSA 2010 model, Sex (Female) has an odds ratio of 0.646. Which statement is correct?

  1. Women are 35% less likely than men to say most people can be trusted
  2. Among people of the same age, women’s odds of trusting are about 35% lower than men’s
  3. The probability that a woman trusts most people is 0.646
  4. Being a woman causes lower trust
TipAnswer

b. (0.646 − 1) × 100 = −35%, and this is a change in odds, adjusted for age. Statement a describes probabilities, which differ by about 11 percentage points (see Question 9). Statement d makes a causal claim that survey data alone cannot support.

6. Which category is “1”?

When you first fit the BSA 2010 model, JASP notes that “Can’t be too careful in dealing with people” has been coded as class 1. What does the model describe?

  1. The probability of trusting most people
  2. The probability of not trusting most people
  3. Nothing: JASP fixes the coding automatically
  4. The mean of SocTrust
TipAnswer

b. Logistic regression models the probability of the category coded 1, and JASP codes the second category as 1. All coefficients would flip sign. Reverse the label order (or recode) so that 1 = “Most people can be trusted”.

7. Where to cut a scale

You need to dichotomise a 1-5 trust scale, and the most common answer is the unlabelled middle value, 3. What is the best practice?

  1. Always group the middle value with the higher values
  2. Try both cut-points and report the one with the smaller p-value
  3. Choose the cut on conceptual grounds before seeing the results, report it, and check whether a reasonable alternative changes the conclusions
  4. Drop everyone who answered 3
TipAnswer

c. In the BSA 2010 self-completion question, the conclusions about age and sex flip depending on where the 3s go (Worksheet 4, Exercise 4.1.1). Choosing the cut by its p-value (b) is a form of p-hacking; dropping a quarter of the sample (d) throws away data and changes who you are studying.

8. Model fit

The BSA 2010 model with age and sex has a Nagelkerke R² of 0.019. Which reading is right?

  1. Age and sex together account for only a very small share of the variation in trust
  2. The model explains 19% of the variation in trust
  3. The coefficients for age and sex are not statistically significant
  4. The model has been fitted incorrectly
TipAnswer

a. Pseudo-R² measures like Nagelkerke’s are rough guides, but 0.019 is very small. A small R² is common for attitudes and does not mean the coefficients are wrong or non-significant: the sex coefficient has p < .001.

9. Back to probabilities

The model predicts that a 20-year-old man has a 47% probability of saying most people can be trusted, and a 20-year-old woman 36%. How big is the sex difference in probability terms?

  1. 35%
  2. About 11 percentage points
  3. 0.646
  4. 1.98
TipAnswer

b. 47% − 36% ≈ 11 percentage points, and the gap is similar at every age. This is the clearest way to tell a general reader how big the difference is. The 35% (a) and 0.646 (c) describe odds, not probabilities.

10. The linear probability model

If you fit a linear regression to the 0/1 SocTrust outcome with age and sex, the coefficient for Sex (Female) is −0.108. What does it mean?

  1. Women are about 10.8 percentage points less likely than men of the same age to say most people can be trusted
  2. Women’s odds of trusting are 10.8% lower
  3. The log-odds of trusting are 0.108 lower for women
  4. Women are 0.108 times as likely to trust
TipAnswer

a. With a 0/1 outcome, a linear regression coefficient is a difference in probability. Here it almost exactly matches the logistic model’s predictions, because all the predicted probabilities sit between 35% and 60%, where the logistic curve is close to a straight line.
