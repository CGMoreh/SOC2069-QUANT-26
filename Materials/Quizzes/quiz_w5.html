Week 5 Self-check Quiz: Uncertainty and inference

Ten short questions to check your understanding after the Week 5 lecture. It takes about 10 minutes. Try each question before opening the answer. The examples use the same data as the lecture and Worksheet 5.

1. Why inference?

Why do we need confidence intervals and p-values at all?

  1. Because survey data always contain mistakes
  2. Because a different random sample would give a different estimate, and we want to say something about the population
  3. Because JASP requires them
  4. Because the mean is not reliable
TipAnswer

b. Every random sample gives a slightly different answer. Inference describes how far a single sample’s estimate is likely to be from the population value.

2. The standard error

What happens to the standard error of a proportion if the sample size is four times larger?

  1. It becomes four times smaller
  2. It becomes about half as large
  3. It stays the same
  4. It becomes larger
TipAnswer

b. The standard error shrinks with the square root of the sample size: √4 = 2.

3. A confidence interval

In the BSA 2010, 45.4% of 1,060 respondents say most people can be trusted (95% CI 42.3% to 48.4%). Which statement is correct?

  1. 95% of respondents gave an answer between 42.3% and 48.4%
  2. We can be 95% confident that between 42.3% and 48.4% of British adults in 2010 would say most people can be trusted
  3. The survey is 95% accurate
  4. The true share is exactly 45.4%
TipAnswer

b. The interval gives a range of plausible values for the population share. It covers sampling error only, not problems with wording, non-response or coverage.

4. What “95%” refers to

In a simulation, 50 random samples each produce a 95% confidence interval. About how many intervals would you expect to contain the true population value?

  1. All 50
  2. About 47 or 48
  3. About 25
  4. It’s impossible to say
TipAnswer

b. Across many samples, 95% of intervals built this way contain the true value (in the lecture’s simulation, 47 of 50). For any single real interval, we cannot know whether it is one of the hits or one of the misses.

5. The null hypothesis

An analyst claims that 20% of households are in poverty; your sample of 500 finds 23%. What is the null hypothesis?

  1. The true share is 23%
  2. The true share is 20%
  3. The true share is not 20%
  4. The sample is biased
TipAnswer

b. The null hypothesis is the “devil’s advocate” position: the claim is correct and our 23% differs from it only by chance.

6. The p-value

For that poverty example, p = 0.09. What does this mean?

  1. There is a 9% chance that the analyst is right
  2. If the true share were 20%, samples of 500 at least this far from 20% would turn up about 9% of the time
  3. The difference is 9 percentage points
  4. We have proved the analyst is wrong
TipAnswer

b. The p-value is calculated assuming the null hypothesis is true; it is not the probability that the null is true. At the conventional 5% level we would not reject the analyst’s claim, but that doesn’t prove it right either.

7. Intervals and p-values

A regression coefficient has a 95% CI of −10.98 to −3.32. What can you say about its p-value?

  1. It is below .05
  2. It is above .05
  3. It is exactly .05
  4. Nothing: CIs and p-values are unrelated
TipAnswer

a. A 95% confidence interval excludes 0 exactly when the two-sided p-value is below .05. (This is the inequality coefficient in the Spirit Level model: p < .001.)

8. Significant but small

Men in the ESS score 0.27 points higher than women on the 0-10 trust scale (95% CI 0.05 to 0.49, p = .015; SD about 2.3). Which description is best?

  1. A large and important gender gap
  2. A statistically significant but small difference: about a tenth of a standard deviation
  3. No real difference, because the gap is small
  4. Men are 27% more trusting
TipAnswer

b. Statistical significance is about how surprising the data would be if there were no difference, not about how big the difference is. Always report the size of the difference and its interval.

9. Not significant

In the BSA 2024, 16-24-year-olds score 0.16 points lower than older adults (95% CI −0.71 to 0.40, p = .58; only 78 young respondents). The much larger Community Life Survey finds a gap of −0.59. What should you conclude?

  1. The BSA 2024 shows that young people’s trust is no different
  2. The BSA 2024 is too imprecise to settle the question; its interval includes both 0 and −0.59
  3. The two surveys contradict each other
  4. The Community Life Survey must be wrong
TipAnswer

b. “Not statistically significant” means “not enough evidence”, not “no difference”. The BSA 2024 interval is compatible with no difference and with the CLS estimate.

10. Odds ratio intervals

In a logistic regression, the odds ratio for women is 0.65 with a 95% CI of 0.51 to 0.83. What does the interval tell you?

  1. Women’s odds of trusting are lower than men’s, and the data are not compatible with no difference
  2. The result is not statistically significant because the interval is wide
  3. Women’s probability of trusting is between 51% and 83%
  4. The interval should be checked against 0
TipAnswer

a. For odds ratios, “no difference” is 1, not 0. The whole interval lies below 1, so p < .05. The values are odds ratios, not probabilities.
